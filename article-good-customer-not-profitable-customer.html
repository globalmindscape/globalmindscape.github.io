When a Good Customer Is Not a Profitable Customer

Rethinking Account Management

← All insights  ·  5 minute read

A large customer is often considered a valuable customer. They generate substantial turnover, place regular orders and may have supported the business for many years.

However, revenue alone does not tell the full story.

Some customers require frequent visits, additional technical support, special delivery arrangements, customised products or extended payment terms. These requirements have a cost. When the cost of serving an account is not properly understood, a business may devote considerable resources to a customer who contributes less profit than expected.

For South African B2B businesses operating with tight margins and limited resources, understanding customer profitability is an important part of effective account management.

Customer loyalty and profitability are different measures

A long-standing customer may be loyal, but loyalty does not automatically make the relationship profitable.

In their 2002 Harvard Business Review article, The Mismanagement of Customer Loyalty, Werner Reinartz and V. Kumar examined information from 16,000 customers across four companies.

Their research found no automatic relationship between customer longevity and profitability. They challenged the assumption that loyal customers necessarily cost less to serve, pay higher prices or generate greater profits.

Their findings show that customer loyalty and customer profitability need to be considered separately.

This is particularly relevant in B2B environments where customer relationships can involve negotiated prices, account-specific service arrangements and ongoing support.

A customer may generate substantial revenue while also requiring significant resources to maintain the relationship.

Understanding the cost of serving a customer

Cost-to-serve refers to the costs associated with supplying and supporting a particular customer.

Depending on the business, these may include:

  • Order processing and administration.
  • Delivery and transport.
  • Frequent or small-volume orders.
  • Special packaging or product handling.
  • Technical assistance.
  • Customer visits and account management.
  • Returns, complaints and rework.
  • Credit management and the cost of extended payment terms.

These costs may be spread across different departments and recorded in different parts of the business.

As a result, management may have a clear view of sales revenue and product costs but a less complete understanding of what it takes to service an individual account.

Why gross margin may not tell the whole story

Gross margin is an important measure, but it does not necessarily reflect every cost associated with serving a customer.

Consider two customers buying from the same supplier.

Customer A places large, regular orders, accepts standard delivery arrangements and pays within agreed terms.

Customer B places frequent small orders, requires special deliveries, requests additional technical support and regularly needs administrative intervention.

Even if both customers generate similar gross margins, the cost of serving them may differ considerably.

This does not automatically make Customer B a poor customer. It means management needs a fuller picture before deciding how to allocate resources or change the commercial arrangement.

Research by Niraj, Gupta and Narasimhan examined customer profitability in a supply chain context and demonstrated the value of considering customer-related costs when evaluating profitability. Their work supports looking beyond sales revenue and product margin when assessing customer relationships.

The importance of customer segmentation

Customer segmentation helps a business decide how much attention and which services different customers should receive.

A business may distinguish between:

  • Strategic accounts: Customers with significant current or future commercial importance who justify a structured relationship and appropriate management attention.
  • Growth accounts: Customers with potential for increased business and opportunities to develop the relationship.
  • Established accounts: Customers who provide consistent business and can be managed through an efficient service approach.
  • Transactional accounts: Customers whose requirements are relatively straightforward and can often be handled through standard processes.

These categories are examples of a practical management approach. Each business should define its own criteria based on its market, products, customer requirements and commercial objectives.

The purpose is not to label customers as good or bad. It is to match the level of service and management attention to the value and potential of the relationship.

Practical implications for business owners and managers

Management can improve account decisions by examining four areas.

1. Understand the full cost of serving customers

Consider the resources used to manage each account. Look beyond product cost and include relevant delivery, administration, support and service requirements.

2. Examine profitability and potential together

Current profitability is important, but future potential also matters. A customer may currently require additional support because the relationship is developing or because a growth opportunity exists.

Management should consider both the present contribution and the realistic future value of the account.

3. Establish appropriate service arrangements

Different customer groups may require different service levels.

Standard ordering and delivery arrangements can improve efficiency for some accounts, while strategic customers may justify more specialised attention.

The service arrangement should be commercially appropriate and clearly understood by both parties.

4. Review account relationships regularly

Customer requirements, purchasing patterns, costs and commercial potential change over time.

Regular reviews help management identify accounts that need additional attention, revised arrangements or a different approach to service.

An illustrative example

Consider a distributor supplying products to two groups of customers.

The first group places predictable orders, accepts standard delivery schedules and requires limited support.

The second group generates similar revenue but regularly requests urgent deliveries, smaller order quantities and additional technical assistance.

Management reviews the two groups and discovers that the second group requires considerably more time and resources.

The business can now examine the reasons for the difference and consider practical changes. These might include agreed order schedules, delivery charges, minimum order quantities or clearly defined service arrangements.

The objective is to improve the commercial relationship while continuing to meet legitimate customer requirements.

What management should take away

Customer relationships should be evaluated using more than turnover and loyalty.

Understanding the cost of serving customers helps management make better decisions about pricing, service levels, account allocation and resource planning.

A large customer can be commercially important, but the business needs to understand what it receives in return for the resources it commits.

Effective account management means developing valuable relationships while maintaining a clear understanding of their commercial contribution.
