The Hidden Cost of Discounting

Understanding the Gap Between Quoted Price and Realised Profit

← All insights  ·  5 minute read

Discounting is a familiar part of business-to-business sales. A customer requests a better price, a competitor submits a lower quotation, or a sales representative offers a concession to secure an order. Each decision may appear reasonable when viewed as an individual transaction.

The difficulty arises when discounting becomes routine and management loses sight of its cumulative effect on profitability.

The price shown on a quotation is not necessarily the amount the business ultimately realises from the transaction. Discounts, rebates, delivery concessions, payment terms and other arrangements can reduce the actual return from a sale.

For South African businesses operating in competitive markets, understanding this difference is an important part of managing commercial performance.

Why price management matters

Pricing has a direct relationship with profitability. In their 1992 Harvard Business Review article, Managing Price, Gaining Profit, Michael V. Marn and Robert L. Rosiello examined the effect of changes in price, volume and costs on operating profit.

Their analysis showed that, for the average company in their model, a 1% improvement in price, assuming volume remained unchanged, could increase operating profit by 11.1%. A 1% increase in sales volume, under the assumptions used, produced a 3.3% increase in operating profit.

These figures are not a forecast for every business. They illustrate the potential financial sensitivity of profit to pricing decisions. The actual effect depends on a company's margins, cost structure, customer response and ability to maintain sales volumes.

The practical lesson is that increasing sales volume does not automatically compensate for lower prices. Management needs to understand the profitability of the transactions being secured.

The difference between quoted price and realised price

A quotation usually records the price offered to the customer. The final transaction may include several additional concessions.

These can include:

  • Discounts applied directly to the invoice.
  • Rebates linked to purchasing volumes.
  • Early-payment incentives.
  • Free or subsidised delivery.
  • Additional services included at no charge.
  • Extended payment terms.
  • Special handling or other customer-specific arrangements.

Each concession has a cost. When these costs are considered separately, their combined effect can be underestimated.

Marn and Rosiello describe a method called the pocket price waterfall. It tracks how the price reduces between the initial list price and the amount the business actually receives from the customer.

The method helps management identify where value is being given away during the sales and fulfilment process.

For example, a business may approve a 5% invoice discount while also absorbing delivery costs and providing additional services. The actual concession is therefore greater than the discount visible on the quotation.

How discounting becomes a management problem

Discounting becomes difficult to control when sales representatives have broad discretion, approval procedures are unclear, or management measures performance mainly through revenue.

A representative working towards a sales target may focus on securing the order. The commercial consequences of the discount may receive less attention, particularly where commission and performance measures reward turnover rather than profitable sales.

Research by Stephan Liozu and Andreas Hinterhuber examined the relationship between pricing approaches, pricing capabilities and company performance. Their study surveyed 1,812 professionals involved in pricing. It found a positive relationship between value-based pricing and firm performance, as well as between pricing capabilities and firm performance. The findings support the importance of developing structured pricing capabilities rather than relying entirely on informal decisions.

This does not mean every discount is inappropriate. Discounts can support customer acquisition, encourage larger orders, respond to competitive conditions or form part of an agreed commercial arrangement.

The important distinction is whether the concession has a clear commercial purpose and whether its financial impact is understood.

Practical implications for business owners and managers

Management can begin by examining five areas.

1. Understand the full transaction price

Look beyond the invoice discount. Include rebates, delivery concessions, payment incentives and additional services when assessing what the business receives from a transaction.

2. Establish discount approval guidelines

Set clear authority levels for approving discounts and other concessions. The limits should reflect the company's margins, pricing strategy and commercial responsibilities.

3. Measure profitable sales

Review sales performance using measures that include gross profit or contribution, rather than relying exclusively on turnover. This gives management a clearer view of the quality of sales being generated.

4. Review customer and product price differences

Compare the prices realised across similar products, customers and order sizes. Significant differences may have valid explanations, but they deserve investigation.

5. Equip sales teams to communicate value

Sales representatives need to understand the value of what they sell and be able to explain it. A price discussion should consider the customer's requirements, the benefits offered and the commercial value of the solution.

An illustrative example

Consider a distributor that offers a customer a discount to secure a large order. The quotation shows the reduced price, but the agreement also includes free delivery, extended payment terms and an additional service.

The order increases monthly turnover, yet the business has not calculated the combined cost of these concessions.

When management reviews the transaction, it discovers that the additional costs have reduced the contribution from the order significantly.

This example illustrates why a sales decision should be assessed against its full commercial impact, rather than the order value alone.

What management should take away

Discounting is a commercial decision. It should be considered in relation to customer value, competitive conditions, margins and the cost of fulfilling the sale.

A business that measures only turnover may overlook the effect of concessions on profitability. By examining the full transaction, setting clear approval rules and monitoring realised prices, management can make better-informed pricing decisions.

The objective is not to eliminate discounting. It is to understand when a concession makes commercial sense and when it weakens the return from a sale.
