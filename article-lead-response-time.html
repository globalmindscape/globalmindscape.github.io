Lead management

How quickly do you answer an enquiry?

Research on more than a million sales leads shows that the first hour matters far more than most businesses think. Here is what it found, and how to check your own response time this week.

← All insights  ·  3 minute read

An enquiry comes in through the website, a WhatsApp message or a call that went to voicemail. Someone means to reply. Then a customer needs attention, a quote is due, and the enquiry sits.

Most owners and sales managers believe they respond quickly. The research suggests most do not.

What the research found

In 2011, Harvard Business Review published a study by James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran and David Elkington. The authors sent test web enquiries to 2,241 US companies and recorded what happened. Separately, they analysed 1.25 million leads handled by 42 US companies.

The test enquiries showed how slowly businesses answer:

  • The average reply took 42 hours, counting only companies that replied within 30 days.
  • 37% replied within an hour.
  • 24% took more than a day.
  • 23% never replied at all.

The lead data showed what the delay costs. Companies that tried to make contact within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as companies that waited even one more hour. They were more than 60 times as likely as companies that waited 24 hours or longer.

How far to trust those numbers

The data is American, it is from 2011, and it covers web enquiries. A South African business that gets most of its enquiries on WhatsApp and by phone should not expect the same multiples. What carries over is the direction. Leads lose value quickly, and most businesses are slower than they believe.

You will also see claims online that you must reply within five minutes, or that speed makes you 100 times more likely to win. Those come from older studies and software company reports. The Harvard study measured hours, not minutes, and it measured qualified leads, not sales.

Why it happens

This is rarely laziness. Enquiries arrive in several places and nobody owns them. Replying to a stranger feels less urgent than serving a customer who already pays you. And when the process depends on memory, memory is the first thing to fail on a busy day.

How to fix it

  1. Find your real number. Take your last 20 enquiries and write down how long each waited for a first reply from a person. Then ask a friend to send three test enquiries through the channels your customers use: a weekday morning, a weekday evening and a weekend. Note how long each takes to get an answer.
  2. Put every enquiry in one list. Website, WhatsApp, phone, email and walk-ins all go in the same place on the same day. A spreadsheet is enough. Use these columns: date and time received, source, name, what they want, who owns it, time of first reply, next step and its date.
  3. Give each enquiry one owner. A named person, not “the team”.
  4. Set a target you can keep. For example, a person replies within one hour during office hours, and first thing the next morning after hours. An automatic message that says when you will reply sets expectations, but it is not the reply. A target you meet is worth more than an ambitious one you ignore.
  5. Review it weekly. Count how many enquiries were answered within target. Then list every enquiry older than three days with no next step. Each one is either a lost opportunity or a closed one, and someone should decide which.

What speed does not fix

Speed does not replace a good answer. A fast reply that is vague or wrong will not win the work. But a good reply that arrives two days late may be talking to a customer who has already moved on.
