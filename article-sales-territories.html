Are Your Sales Territories Working for the Business?

Aligning Sales Coverage with Market Opportunity

← All insights  ·  5 minute read

Sales territories are often organised around geography, historical arrangements or the number of customers assigned to each representative. These approaches can provide a practical starting point, but they do not necessarily create a fair or commercially effective distribution of sales opportunities.

Two representatives may cover areas of similar size while facing very different market conditions. One may have a concentration of established businesses and high-potential accounts. Another may cover a much larger area with fewer suitable prospects and significantly greater travel requirements.

The result can be an uneven distribution of opportunity, workload and selling time.

For businesses that rely on field sales, territory design deserves regular management attention.

What makes a sales territory effective?

A sales territory is more than a geographic boundary. It is an allocation of customers, prospects and sales responsibilities.

An effective territory structure should consider:

  • The number and potential value of customers.
  • The concentration of suitable prospects.
  • Existing account requirements.
  • Travel distances and time.
  • The frequency of customer contact required.
  • The capacity and experience of the representative.
  • The commercial objectives of the business.

Equal geographic areas do not necessarily represent equal commercial opportunity. Likewise, assigning the same number of accounts to each representative does not guarantee a balanced workload.

Management needs to consider both the opportunity available and the effort required to develop and service it.

What does research tell us?

Sales territory design has been studied extensively in sales force management.

In their 1983 paper, Sales Territory Alignment: A Review and Model, Andris Zoltners and Prabhakant Sinha developed a framework for evaluating territory alignment. Their work examined the characteristics of effective territory structures and presented a model for designing and comparing them.

In a later review, Zoltners and Sinha discussed the development of territory alignment methods over three decades. They reported that their firm's work had been applied in more than 1,500 projects across over 500 companies and estimated that good alignment could improve sales by 2% to 7% compared with average alignment.

This is an estimate reported by the authors based on their experience. It should not be treated as a guaranteed result for an individual business.

Research by Bernd Skiera and Sönke Albers also examined the relationship between territory alignment and profit contribution. Their COSTA model considers sales response, selling time and travel time when allocating sales effort and assigning accounts to territories. Their work highlights the importance of considering profitability rather than simply balancing territory size or workload.

Together, these studies demonstrate that territory design is a commercial management decision, not merely an administrative exercise.

The risks of relying on historical territory arrangements

Territories often remain unchanged for years. During that time, customer locations, market conditions, competitors and business priorities can change.

A territory that once worked well may gradually become less effective.

Common warning signs include:

Uneven opportunity

Some representatives have access to a greater concentration of potential customers or larger accounts, while others have limited opportunities within their assigned areas.

Excessive travel

Representatives spend a substantial part of their working time travelling between customers, reducing the time available for prospecting, meetings and follow-up.

Inconsistent customer coverage

Important customers receive insufficient attention because the representative's workload is already stretched.

Unclear account ownership

Customers are approached by multiple representatives, or responsibility for following up opportunities becomes uncertain.

Limited market development

Representatives concentrate on familiar customers and established routes while potential opportunities in other parts of their territories remain unexplored.

These issues can affect sales productivity, customer experience and the cost of maintaining the sales operation.

Practical implications for business owners and sales managers

A territory review should begin with reliable information about customers, opportunities and the work required to service them.

1. Examine the actual market opportunity

Review the number and type of potential customers in each territory. Consider industry concentration, customer suitability, account size and potential demand.

Historical sales figures are useful, but they do not necessarily reveal the full opportunity available.

2. Review workload and capacity

Consider the time required for customer visits, prospecting, travel, quotations, administration and follow-up.

The purpose is to understand how much time representatives have available for productive selling activity.

3. Segment customers

Not every account requires the same level of attention. Some may justify regular face-to-face contact, while others can be managed through scheduled telephone calls, email or digital communication.

Customer segmentation helps management allocate time according to commercial importance and service requirements.

4. Consider travel and geographic coverage

Look at the practical routes representatives follow and the time spent travelling. Grouping accounts sensibly can reduce unnecessary travel and create more time for customer engagement.

5. Review territories regularly

Territory design should respond to changes in the market and the business. New customers, lost accounts, product changes and shifts in strategic priorities can all affect the suitability of existing arrangements.

An illustrative example

Imagine a business supplying building materials to contractors and hardware stores.

One representative covers a compact urban area with a high concentration of potential customers. Another covers a much larger rural area with fewer prospects and longer travel distances.

Both representatives have similar sales targets and are expected to make the same number of customer visits.

The arrangement appears equal on paper, but the commercial conditions are different.

A territory review could examine customer potential, travel requirements, account service needs and available selling time. Management could then adjust account allocation, visit expectations or territory boundaries to create a more practical structure.

The appropriate solution would depend on the business's actual customer and market information.

What management should take away

Territory design affects how a business deploys its sales resources. It influences which customers receive attention, how much time representatives spend travelling and where the business concentrates its selling effort.

A territory structure should reflect market opportunity, customer requirements and available capacity.

Regular reviews help management identify imbalances and adapt the sales operation as the business changes.

The objective is to put sales effort where it can make the greatest commercial contribution.
