Commercial performance

What a discount really costs

A 5% discount feels small when a deal is close. McKinsey’s 2025 research shows how much extra selling it takes to pay for it, and this article gives you a way to work out your own number.

← All insights  ·  3 minute read

A customer asks for a better price. The deal is close, month-end is near, and a 5% discount feels like a small price for a signed order.

It rarely is. A discount comes straight off profit, and the extra sales needed to pay for it are larger than most people expect.

What the research says

McKinsey’s 2025 review of B2B sales performance, by Darius Bates, Yasir Mirza, Medha Medha and Thomas Lamoureux, puts two numbers side by side. A 1% price increase typically lifts operating profit by 6 to 14%. A 5% price cut needs, on average, a 21% rise in volume just to break even.

A 2024 McKinsey article gives an example. A B2B IT distributor cut prices for 15 large accounts for two months. Volume did not rise enough to make up for the lost margin, so the company went back to its higher-margin approach. The 2025 article also lists giving discounts too easily to win deals as one of the common pricing mistakes.

Work out your own break-even

You do not have to rely on averages. The calculation is simple and uses your own margin. Take the discount as a percentage of the price. Take your margin as a percentage of the price after variable costs, which means the cost of the goods, delivery and anything else that rises with each sale. Then:

Extra volume needed = discount ÷ (margin minus discount)

This is our own arithmetic, not a research finding. This is what it gives:

Margin after variable costs5% discount10% discount15% discount
20%33% more100% more300% more
30%20% more50% more100% more
40%14% more33% more60% more

Read it like this. If you keep 30 cents of every rand after variable costs, a 10% discount means you must sell 50% more to end up with the same money. At a 20% margin you must sell twice as much. McKinsey’s 21% average is close to what a 30% margin gives for a 5% cut.

Some discounts are worth giving. If a customer commits to more volume, pays faster or signs a longer contract, you get something back. The problem is the discount that buys nothing.

Find out what you actually keep

  1. Pick 20 to 30 recent invoices or accepted quotes. Spread them across your customers, not only the best ones.
  2. Write down the list price and every reduction. Include the discount on the quote, rebates and volume bonuses, early-payment discounts, delivery or installation you absorbed, and free extras such as samples or training.
  3. Subtract to find what you kept. Show it as a percentage of the list price. Consultants call this the pocket price.
  4. Sort the results. By customer, by the person who agreed the discount, and by order size.
  5. Look for patterns. Do the biggest orders get the biggest discounts, or the loudest customers? Do some people discount far more than others on similar deals? Which discounts bought something back?

Rules that help

These are practical suggestions, not findings from the research.

  • Set discount bands, with a higher level of approval for each step up.
  • Make every concession buy something: volume, faster payment or a longer commitment.
  • Record the discount on every quote, so it can be counted later.
  • Review the numbers monthly, by customer and by person.

Where the discounting happens differs between sales models. In field sales it is often agreed on the spot, at the customer’s counter. In quote and proposal businesses it is written into the quote. In both, what is not recorded cannot be managed.
