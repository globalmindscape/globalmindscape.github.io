Commercial performance

What you actually keep from a sale

The price on the quote is not the price you earn. McKinsey research shows why small changes in price matter so much to profit, and this article shows you how to calculate your own numbers.

← All insights  ·  3 minute read

Ask a business owner what they charged a customer and you will usually get the invoice total. Ask what they kept after discounts, rebates, early payment terms, free delivery and the extras thrown in to close the deal, and the answer takes longer.

The gap between those two numbers is where a lot of profit goes missing. Much of it never appears on a report.

The price waterfall

The idea goes back to a 1992 Harvard Business Review article by Michael Marn and Robert Rosiello. They called it the price waterfall. You start with the list price and take off each discount and concession in turn until you reach what the business keeps. That final figure is the pocket price.

In 2003, McKinsey published a worked example from a lighting supplier. Its average invoice price was 32.8% below the standard list price. Payments that never appear on the invoice took off another 16.3 percentage points. The average pocket price was about half the list price: 100 on the price list, 67.2 on the invoice and 50.9 in the supplier’s pocket.

The off-invoice items in that article included cash discounts for early payment, rebates, co-operative advertising, promotional programmes and freight. Leakage like this sits off the invoice, where it is easy to miss.

Why a small change in price matters so much

The same article, by Marn, Roegner and Zawada, used typical figures for companies in the S&P 1500. It found that a 1% rise in price, with volume holding steady, would raise operating profit by 8%. That is nearly 50% more than the effect of cutting variable costs by 1%, and more than three times the effect of selling 1% more. It also works in reverse: a 1% fall in price cuts operating profit by the same 8%.

Those are large listed companies, so do not expect exactly 8% in your business. The size of the effect depends on your margin. If operating profit is 10% of sales, then 1% of sales gained in price is 10% of your profit. At 5% operating profit it is 20%. The thinner the margin, the more each point of price matters.

One caution. These figures assume volume holds when price moves, and sometimes it will not. The point is not that you should raise prices. The point is to know where your price is going before you decide what to do about it.

Work out your own pocket price

  1. Pick 20 to 30 recent invoices or accepted quotes. Choose a spread of customers, not only the best ones.
  2. Write down the list price, then every reduction. Include the discount on the quote, volume bonuses and rebates, early-payment discounts, delivery or installation you absorbed, price matching, and free extras such as samples, set-up or training.
  3. Subtract to find the pocket price. Express it as a percentage of list price.
  4. Sort the results three ways. By customer, by the person who gave the discount, and by order size.
  5. Look for patterns. Do the biggest orders get the biggest discounts, or the loudest customers? Do some people discount far more than others on similar deals? Which concessions bought you something back, such as volume, faster payment or a longer contract, and which were simply asked for?

What usually helps

Once you can see the pattern, a few simple rules usually do most of the work. These are practical suggestions, not findings from the research.

  • Set discount bands, with a higher level of approval for each step up.
  • Make every concession buy something. If a customer wants a lower price, they give volume, faster payment or a longer commitment.
  • Record the discount on every quote, so it can be counted later.
  • Review the numbers monthly, by customer and by person.

The way the leakage happens differs between sales models. In field sales, concessions are often agreed in the moment, at the customer’s counter. In quote and proposal businesses they are written into the quote. In both, what is not recorded cannot be managed.
