Sales conversion

Why deals go quiet

Many deals are not lost to a competitor. They fade after a good meeting and a quote that was well received. Recent research explains why, and what you can do about it.

← All insights  ·  3 minute read

The meeting went well. The customer liked the quote. Then you hear “let me think about it”, and after that, nothing.

It is tempting to blame price or a competitor. The research points somewhere else.

Many lost deals are not lost to a competitor

For their 2022 book The Jolt Effect, Matthew Dixon and Ted McKenna analysed about 2.5 million recorded sales conversations from several dozen companies. They found that between 40 and 60% of deals are lost to the customer doing nothing. Dixon’s reading is that much of this comes from customers who worry about making an expensive mistake, not from customers who are happy with how things are.

The customer often has to convince other people

In 2025, Gartner published a survey of 632 B2B buyers, carried out in August and September 2024. It found that 74% of buying teams show “unhealthy conflict” while deciding. That means team members want different things, disagree on what to do, or are overruled by someone outside the team. The buying groups in the survey ranged from 5 to 16 people across as many as four functions.

The effect on the deal was clear. Groups that reached agreement were 2.5 times more likely to report a high-quality deal. When sellers shaped their material around what the whole group needed, agreement improved by 20%. When it focused on one person’s priorities, agreement fell by 59%.

What this means for a smaller business

The survey covers B2B buyers in general, and a group of 5 to 16 people will not describe most small customers. The pattern still applies in a smaller form. Your contact often has to win over a partner, a finance person or a board. We think that is a common reason a good deal goes quiet, but that is our reading, not a finding from the research.

What to do about it

These are practical suggestions, not findings from the research.

  1. Ask who else has to agree. Early on, ask: “Who else will need to be comfortable with this?” Write their names in your pipeline record.
  2. Treat “let me think about it” as a question. Ask what they are weighing up. The answer is usually a worry: the cost, the disruption, being blamed if it goes wrong. You can only deal with a worry once you know what it is.
  3. Write something they can forward. Send a short summary for the customer to pass on: the problem it solves, what it costs, what happens first and what the risks are. Write it for the person who has not met you.
  4. Make the first step smaller. A defined first stage, a clear scope and a review date make the decision feel safer than one large commitment.
  5. Agree the next step before the conversation ends. Name it and put a date on it. If the customer will not agree to one, mark the deal as at risk.

Keep a record of why deals end

Most businesses record a deal as won or lost. Add a third outcome, no decision, and count it separately. If 40% of your lost deals fall there, you have a different problem from a business that loses on price, and you will not see the difference unless you record it.

Then check your pipeline once a week for deals that have been quiet for more than two weeks. Contact each one or close it. A deal with no next step is not a forecast. It is a hope.
